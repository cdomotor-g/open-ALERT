The set-up

One antenna, split two ways, so both computers hear exactly the same bursts. Each has its own stick of the same model, its own Flood-Net ingest token and its own receivers in Flood-Net. Flood-Net keeps one copy of each reading — whichever arrives first — but records every copy on the Reception Map, so the two can be compared burst for burst. Halfway through, swap the sticks: no two sticks are equally good, and swapping cancels that out.

Passes if

  • it decodes at least 98% of the bursts the Pi does, over both weeks
  • no channel ever drops samples
  • no channel takes over 70% of one core in the worst hour (Decoder busy), and all together under 70% of the computer
  • the processor stays under 70 °C
  • no restarts of its own, and no check-in gap the Pi does not share (a gap both have is the network's)

Takes

  • the benchmark first: about four minutes
  • a couple of hours on the bench to bring it up (allow days the first time: the firmware is new)
  • an hour on site to fit the splitter
  • two weeks running — ideally with some rain in them
  • an hour to read off and compare the numbers

Costs

The cost follows the computer you pick below.

The firmware is new. open-ALERT's firmware/ takes RPi ALERT's signed release unchanged and wraps it three ways: a package for any Debian 13 or Ubuntu 24.04 computer, the same in a container with no privileges for Torizon OS, and disk images. As at 13 November 2026 the package is built and its own checks pass on Debian 13 and Ubuntu 24.04 with stand-ins for the radio, on one test computer that has no USB; the container has only been tried with stand-ins for the radio, on one PC: never on Torizon OS, on an ARM computer or with a real stick; the disk images are still being written; nothing is released; and nothing has yet been run with a real radio stick on a computer that is not a Raspberry Pi. The trial is where that gets proved. Releases will appear at github.com/cdomotor-g/open-ALERT/releases (none on that date). The firmware's own notes are always the current word on what has been tested where.

Which computer

Pick the computer to trial. Any of these will do: the shopping list, the power notes and the steps below follow your pick. The Other computers page compares them in full, with photographs and where to buy each one. The default is the cheapest way to learn, an N150 mini PC from an Australian shop, for the bench; the OnLogic CL260 (for a 12 V or 24 V site) and the Lenovo ThinkEdge (where IT wants a brand it already buys) are the ones to buy for real.

The one-line verdict: buy an N150-class fanless PC with a TPM (the CL260 for a battery site, the ThinkEdge where the brand matters), prove it on the bench with a A$300 mini PC first if you like, and keep the Verdin AM62 and the Luna out of the order book.

Every computer is rated below for your channels with the corrected sums: all the channels together against the whole computer, and the busiest channel against one core, because each channel is decoded on one core. Speed is how much decoding it does as a multiple of a Pi 4; no published benchmark runs this program, so every figure but the Pi 4's is a stand-in until the benchmark in step 0 settles it.

Each computer has two lines: its figures, then what it is built with and what the hardware review says of it. Under "all together" and "busiest channel" the first figure is RPi ALERT's measurement alone and the second the same work on a busy computer. Green is comfortable, amber the edge, red dropping bursts. Change the channels and traffic under Will it keep up? and the table follows. The rule for buying with headroom for ALERT2: at least four cores, each at least 2.5 times a Pi 4 core. The N150 and N355 classes meet it at their planning figure of about 3 a core (the lowest published benchmark puts an N150 core at about 2.4, just under: run the benchmark before buying several); a Pi 4 and the Beagle boards do not. "Probably meets it" is for a computer whose processor the review has no figure for. A temperature marked "assumed" is not the maker's: no rating was read.

If you still want the Verdin — the AM62s, with the corrected prices, the right heatsink and the iMX95 beside them

The old trial (the Mallow trial) chose a Toradex Verdin AM62 Quad on a Mallow carrier. The hardware review found it should not be run as written: the module's price had roughly tripled, its heatsink did not fit, and its speed had never been measured. It is not recommended; this is for those who still want it.

Will it keep up?

Put in the channels your Pi listens on to see what the builder's model expects of the Pi 4 and of your pick — then let the trial say what really happens. On a busy Pi 4, today's work is about 0.55–0.6 of a Pi 4 core a channel in a 1.44 MHz window, 0.25–0.5 at 960 kHz and 0.15–0.25 at 240 kHz; four channels on one stick take about 2.2–2.5 Pi 4 cores, 55–62% of a Pi 4, in busy to heavy traffic. Those are the top of each range: by RPi ALERT's measurement alone, with nothing else loading the computer, the same four channels take about 1.4 Pi 4 cores, 36%. That is why the table below shows the Pi 4 at 36–55% on a busy day.

Where the figures come from: the work between bursts is RPi ALERT's own Raspberry Pi 4 figure, and the higher end is what that Pi 4 showed while busy with eight channels and a screen, so it may read high. What each burst costs was timed on a PC and multiplied by 4 to 5 to stand for a Pi 4 (review report U2): an estimate until a Pi 4 runs the benchmark.

What would help most

One software change is worth more than any hardware: not trying the older-ALERT decoder first on a channel known to carry ALERT2. It takes a full ALERT2 channel from about 1.2 to about 0.4 Pi 4 cores. It is planned in RPi ALERT, not shipped (review report U2), so nothing on this page counts on it. Two lighter ways of decoding are built and off by default. Resting a channel between bursts (RPi ALERT 0.18) missed some of the weakest bursts in its first field check. The Light decoder (0.19) has had no field check at all. So a Pi 4 keeps filtering all the time, and no sizing should lean on either.

On ALERT2: a channel whose time slots are all used needs about 1.1–1.3 Pi 4 cores of work for its messages alone, and 1.2–1.8 once the cost of listening in the stick's window is added (review report R9): the box above shows that fuller figure for your own channels. All of it lands on one core, which is more than any Pi 4-class core has. Both figures were timed on another computer and scaled to a Pi 4, so they are estimates. Real networks run far below that (20–31 messages a minute). RPi ALERT and Flood-Net work from version 1.1 of the ALERT2 radio specification (AirLink); version 1.2 (January 2025) adds ways of sending that the program cannot hear yet, and optional encryption — software and access questions, not hardware (report R9).

Results sheet

Week 1: stick A on the Pi, stick B on the trial computer. Week 2: swapped. Counts are for the whole week; the verdict adds the two weeks together. A sheet saved by the earlier trial still reads the same: its "AM62" columns are the trial computer's.

If it won't

It won't start from the USB stick, or the stick is refused
Check the maker's page for the operating system it supports, and that the stick was written as a bootable disk. Write down what the firmware screen says; if you change a Secure Boot setting to get the bench build in, write that down too and tell IT before it goes on a network.
The radio stick isn't found
lsusb should list a Realtek RTL2838 device. The package keeps the kernel's digital-TV drivers off the sticks; if one was already holding it, unplug and replug the stick, or reboot. With a Verdin, check the container was given the USB bus. On a computer with one USB port, use a powered hub.
The benchmark says the computer is slow, or Decoder busy is near 100%, or channels drop samples
It is doing more than it can with RPi ALERT's decoder as it is. Take channels off until it settles, and write down how many it managed — that is a result in itself. Then look at what would help most, and at the Other computers page for a faster computer.
Hot: the processor over 70 °C
A fanless computer in the sun throttles. Check it has air round it and is not in direct sun; compare the maker's temperature rating. A throttled processor decodes less.
The Pi got worse after the splitter
Expected, by about 3.5 dB — compare with the baseline you wrote down. If a weak station drops out altogether, note it; take the splitter out when the trial ends.
Both computers lost the network at the same time
That is the network's gap, not the trial computer's: only a gap the Pi does not share counts against it.

When the trial ends

  • Take the splitter out to give the Pi its full signal back — or keep both running.
  • If the trial computer comes off, revoke its ingest token on Flood-Net's Admin tab and remove its receivers.
  • Put the results, and the benchmark file, in open-ALERT's or RPi ALERT's issues so the builder's speed figures can use real numbers.
