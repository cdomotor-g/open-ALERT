How a base station works — from the air to Flood-Net

A base station is an antenna, one or more cheap SDR sticks and a small computer running RPi ALERT (open source, MIT). The sticks don't decode anything: they hand the computer a slice of the band, and software decodes every channel in it. Tap any part to see what it does and which code does it.

How the parts fit together physically

Three ways to get a computer: a single board (a Raspberry Pi — everything on one board), a module on a carrier (the processor, memory and eMMC on a small module that plugs into a carrier board with the connectors — the carrier is the part you can design yourself), or a boxed computer (an industrial PC or mini PC, finished and certified). The SDR either hangs off USB or sits in an M.2 slot.

Can the computer keep up?

Two limits, and both have to hold: all the channels together against the whole computer, and each channel against one core — a channel is decoded on one core and cannot use more.

How far to trust these figures. They come from RPi ALERT's own notes on one Raspberry Pi 4, measured while it was busy with eight channels and a screen, so they may read high. What a burst or an ALERT2 frame costs was timed on a PC and scaled to a Pi 4. No other computer here has run the decoder: each one's speed is an estimate set against a Raspberry Pi 4. The benchmark in firmware/bench runs RPi ALERT's own decoder on the computer in front of you: that is how to settle it on a real one.

The sticks and their windows

Each stick takes the narrowest window that holds its channels; every channel's decoder works through the whole window, so keep each stick's channels close together.

Build it

A board of your own — later, and only for a reason

Buy off the shelf first. The hardware review found that what takes a base station through an IT security review is its operating system, its software and its supplier, not the name on the board. So the first step away from a Raspberry Pi is a small PC from the Other computers page, not a new circuit board. Build a board only if an approval or a site then fails for a concrete reason that a carrier board fixes.

  1. Off the shelf first. Pick a computer, run the benchmark on it, and prove it beside the Pi with the side-by-side trial. Keep the radio a USB stick that can be swapped: the RTL-SDR Blog V4 is no longer made, and no other radio is a straight swap.
  2. Make the decoder lighter before the hardware bigger. Three changes, at three stages. Done: a burst's decode used to re-filter the window for each of 45 guesses; filtering once per carrier guess and nudging afterwards (RPi ALERT's default since 0.17) takes a third of the time — 33–34% on the first base station's Pi 4 — and 14 of 11,628 live bursts read differently (0.12%). Built, but off: between bursts every channel filters the stick's whole window all the time; filtering a channel only around a burst (0.18) costs 4 times less in the narrowest window and 14 times less in the widest while nothing is on the air (measured on a PC), but its first live check missed some of the weakest bursts, so it stays off by default and nothing here is sized on it. Planned, not built: reading a frame as ALERT2 straight away on a channel known to carry it, instead of trying it as an older burst first — worth more for ALERT2 than any hardware: a channel with every slot used falls from about 1.2 Pi 4 cores to about 0.4. None of this brings a busy site within reach of a small module: with today's defaults one channel in a 1.44 MHz window already takes about a whole core of a module half as fast as a Pi 4.
  3. Decide whether there is a reason. The reasons that count: a wide DC input, a TPM, a watchdog that restarts a stuck computer, surge protection, a battery-backed clock, the radio on the board. If the off-the-shelf computer passed its IT review and suits the site, stop here. A board of your own also makes you the supplier who answers for it in law.
  4. Choose what to start from. A module on a carrier, so the hard part (the processor and its memory) is bought. Four places to start:
    • The Raspberry Pi Compute Module 5 IO board. Its KiCad drawing files can be downloaded and the module is fast. It is still a Raspberry Pi; check the licence file in the download.
    • SMARC 2.1. A standard shape of module that several makers build, with NXP's i.MX 95 from more than one of them. No Australian distributor was found.
    • Toradex Mallow. Its schematics and parts list download freely, and its full drawing project sits behind a form. None carries an open-source licence ("All rights are reserved"): get written permission before building on them. Only Toradex makes the modules, and the review does not recommend the AM62 one.
    • Olimex's open iMX8MP module. An open licence that lets you make your own, with KiCad files. About two-thirds of a Raspberry Pi 4, a small maker, no Australian stockist.
    An RTL-SDR on an M.2 card, which the builder above lists as a design to commission, belongs here too: nobody sells one.
  5. Design it, test it, and make it lawful to sell. A carrier of four to six layers and one or two rounds of prototypes. Then, to sell it in Australia: a supplier's declaration of conformity, emissions testing to AS/NZS CISPR 32 at an accredited laboratory, the Regulatory Compliance Mark (RCM), and an Australian responsible supplier, because an overseas company cannot register as one.
