Plan ALERT and ALERT2 flood-warning sites — field stations, repeaters and base stations. Say what each must do; see the hardware that fits, open and closed, budget to industrial; then what your choices cost and commit you to.

How it works

Every site in an ALERT network does one of three jobs. A field station measures rain or river level and sends each reading by VHF radio; a repeater on a hilltop hears field stations and sends their readings on; a base station receives the bursts and forwards them — to Flood-Net, a council's SCADA, or the Bureau.

Four steps for each site

  1. Needs. Answer what the site must do: the protocol, what it measures, its power, where the electronics live, how much reliability matters against cost, whether it must be open source.
  2. Requirements. The answers become a list of requirements — each a must or a should, each traced to the answer it came from.
  3. Hardware. Each part of the site — the controller, the radio, the antenna, the battery — lists every catalogued option, sorted so the ones that meet the requirements come first. Those that don't say why. The supporting parts a choice drags in (coax, arrestor, a supply, a boot card, an adapter) are added for you.
  4. Result. The bill of materials, the power budget with the battery and panel sized for it, whether every requirement is met, and the consequences: the firmware and who maintains it, what is closed, licensing, the skills it needs, the life of each part, the maintenance it commits you to.

Add as many sites as the network has — each with a quantity — and the Network tab adds them up and checks that what the field sends, the repeaters relay and the bases receive actually meet.

The assumptions behind the numbers

  • Prices are indicative, in Australian dollars, gathered in October 2026 from Australian retailers where possible and converted at US$0.69 and €0.61 to the dollar otherwise. Most ALERT equipment is quote-only: where a public planning figure exists (Flood-Net's ALERT2 transition plan) it is used and labelled an estimate; where none does, the price is blank — type your quote into the bill of materials and every total follows. Each price shows its basis: retail, list, estimate, used, quote.
  • GST. Prices show including GST by default; switch it off on the Network tab. Planning figures quoted ex-GST are grossed up.
  • Power. Each part's average draw, from its datasheet. A field station's radio is powered only to send: its average is its transmit draw for about half a second per send, times the sends on a wet day you set. A repeater's receiver listens all day. A 12 V → 5 V converter loses about 12%.
  • Battery. Capacity = daily energy × days of autonomy ÷ (12 V × usable depth of discharge × 0.85). Usable depth: 50% for lead-acid, 80% for lithium iron phosphate.
  • Solar. Panel = daily energy × 1.2 ÷ (worst-month peak-sun-hours × 0.75 with an MPPT controller, 0.65 with PWM). South-east Queensland's winter gives about 3.5 sun-hours on a tilted panel.
  • Heat. A sealed box in full Queensland sun runs 20–25 °C over the air: about 65 °C inside on a hot day (an estimate, not a measurement). That is what "sealed enclosure in full sun" checks parts against; a sun shield or vented box takes 10–15 °C off.
  • Labour. $700 a person-day and $700 a crew-day of travel (ex GST), as in Flood-Net's ALERT2 plan: a field station takes a crew of two a day (a day and a half with a level sensor), a repeater a day and a half (not counting tower work), a base a day plus a day per receive chain; one site visit a year. Change the rates, or switch labour off, on the Network tab.
  • Grades. 1 hobby · 2 consumer, works if looked after · 3 prosumer or light industrial · 4 industrial · 5 proven in hydromet and utility field use.

What the law asks (Australia)

  • A transmitter on an ALERT channel runs under an ACMA apparatus licence, normally held by the network owner — the Bureau, a council, a water authority. An amateur licence does not cover it.
  • The transmitter must be compliant for Australian land-mobile use (AS/NZS 4295) and carry the RCM. Cheap handhelds such as the Quansheng UV-K5 and Baofeng UV-5R are not: on the bench, into a dummy load, they are fine; on a licensed channel they are an offence, with penalties up to two years' imprisonment for unlicensed operation.
  • Receiving needs no licence: a base station or a survey kit can listen with an SDR stick.
  • Mains wiring past a plug-in supply is a licensed electrician's job; climbing a tower is a qualified rigger's.

Not legal advice: check with the network owner and ACMA before transmitting.

The software in this ecosystem

  • RPi ALERT (MIT) — a Raspberry Pi base-station OS: RTL-SDR sticks (several ALERT1 channels each), Quansheng radios on the ALERT firmware, ELPRO ERT-A2 (ALERT2), GPS; forwards to Flood-Net.
  • quansheng_alert_v3 (Apache-2.0) — turns a Quansheng UV-K5 V3 or UV-K1 into a receive-only ALERT1 decoder on USB-C; quansheng_alert for the older DP32G030 radios.
  • FW-LAB Receiver (ALERT1v3) — an RTL-SDR and GNU Radio ALERT receiver with MQTT and a web UI.
  • Flood-Net — the network's web app: the Serial Monitor in Chrome reads the same receivers from a desk PC, and its ingest takes HTTPS, MQTT and the Bureau's HFEM lines.
  • ALERT_PACKETS — encode and decode ALERT frames by hand: the test bench for any encoder you build.

Adding hardware

The catalogue is plain JavaScript in site/data/, one file per family, each part a short record: where it fits, what it can do, its price and where the price came from, its power, temperature range and grade, its firmware, and its sources. Add a part, run npm test, and open a pull request — the tests check every record and every preset.

Specifications are from datasheets and vendor pages; confirm them before you buy. The catalogue is a starting point, not an endorsement. Corrections are welcome as issues on GitHub.
